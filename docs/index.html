Story Studio

A place for students to write their own stories, with an AI writing partner that helps them think, but never writes the story for them.

Nobody Argued

On Monday the principal announced that every essay at Westbrook High would now be written with an AI. Ms. Okafor, who had taught English there for twenty years, said nothing in the staff meeting. That evening she sat at her kitchen table with a stack of last year's essays, reading them one by one, looking for the moment each student had stopped writing like anyone else.

Nobody argued.

Writing partner

Write the next paragraph for me.
The wording should come from you. This next section needs to bridge the gap between Ms. Okafor's private observation and the new reality at Westbrook. You could focus on a specific, "imperfect" sentence in an old essay to show what she's afraid of losing, or jump to the sterile silence in her classroom when the students first open their laptops. Which feels more vital to your concept: the specific beauty of the old work or the atmosphere of the new requirement?

The story on the left belongs to the student. The partner on the right helps but does not take over.

The problem

AI can now write a whole story in seconds. When a student lets it, they skip the part that actually teaches writing: working out what to say and how to say it. What they hand in may not really be theirs.

Banning AI doesn't solve this, because students will use it anyway. Story Studio tries something else: an AI partner that is genuinely useful to a writer while leaving the writing to the student.

Who it's for

Students

University students writing short stories, such as a teaching scenario, a story that carries a lesson, or a piece of near-future fiction, who want help without handing the work over.

Teachers and researchers

People studying how students write with AI. They can see how a class used the partner, without seeing anyone's name.

A quick walkthrough

  1. Start a story

    Open Story Studio, choose Student and type your name. No password needed. Say what the story is about, write its first line, and give it a title if you like.

  2. Write

    Your story sits on the left like a sheet of paper. Your writing partner sits on the right, and you can hide it whenever you want to write alone. Earlier versions are kept, so you can always go back.

  3. Ask for ideas

    Stuck on what happens next? The partner sets out a few different directions and lets you choose. It doesn't pick one for you.

    Can you help me brainstorm what could happen next?
    One direction might follow Ms. Okafor as she attempts to "smuggle" human voice back into the curriculum using those old essays as a guide. Another could focus on a student who feels the loss of their own writing style once the AI takes over. You could also look at how the school's atmosphere shifts as the policy's effects become visible. Which of these consequences interests you most?

  4. Ask it to write for you

    It won't. It says briefly that the words should be yours, then helps you work out what the passage needs to do, like the example at the top of this page.

  5. Quick questions get quick answers

    Spelling, grammar or what a word means: you get the answer straight away, not a question in its place.

    How do you spell accommodate?
    It is spelled "accommodate," with two "c"s and two "m"s. Are you planning to use it to describe how Ms. Okafor tries to adjust to the principal's new mandate, or for something else?

  6. For researchers

    Choose Researcher instead to set up a study, compare groups of students, and see how each group used the partner. Every student appears only as a code, like Participant_07.
